Downloaded 24 public OP18 card images from [OPDeckGuide](https://opdeckguide.com/cards-list/OP18/) and identified 14 with the same visible `SAMPLE` watermark. ## Current conclusion

The initial model and classical tests did not meet a clean, faithful result standard. GPU diffusion removed the word but changed the anatomy. The latest source-only gradient reconstruction, described below, removes the readable word while retaining the original creature's structure. Its repaired band remains somewhat soft because the watermark and lossy WebP have destroyed detail.

The central obstacle is a precise watermark matte: exact shape, alignment, opacity, and edge color. A model asked to replace the whole word must invent too much recognizable anatomy. More UI work would not address this. The next algorithm spike should use an accurately registered, same-export image collection or a known watermark layer, then reserve inpainting for truly unrecoverable pixels.

## Follow-up: stronger de-whitening

With the aligned letter silhouette, increasing uniform white-overlay opacity from 0.65 to 0.70 reduces visible brightness, but pushes 10.6% of masked pixels below zero in at least one recovered color channel. At opacity 0.75, 73.1% clip. A pixel-wise cap based on the darkest observed channel avoids this clipping. The best visual compromise from this sweep is [`opacity_adaptive_0.72_repair_1.png`](archive/root-images/opacity_adaptive_0.72_repair_1.png): darker interiors with the lower head still recognizable, followed by narrow classical edge repair. The letter shapes remain detectable as darker patches. Estimating a smooth opacity from colors interpolated outside the letters was also tested; that baseline was too inaccurate across teeth, smoke, and fur and worsened the result.

## Follow-up: isolated outline repair

Tested normal-direction interpolation across 2-, 3-, and 4-pixel bands around the registered letter silhouette (`normal_edge_repair.py`). It reduces a few bright edge pixels, but blurs or smears artwork along the strokes and leaves the letter geometry visible. Also fitted edge-only white-overlay opacity from colors sampled on both sides of the stroke (`outline_alpha_fit.py`). The fitted opacity was too low along the bright outline, restoring a clearly white `SAMPLE` contour. Neither improves on `archive/root-images/opacity_adaptive_0.72_repair_1.png`.

The next useful experiment is a separate outline mask based on residual brightness and color in the original and de-whitened images, rather than a fixed ring around the fill silhouette. Repair that mask locally with patch-based or guided interpolation, then visually inspect crossings of teeth, fur, and smoke. Automatic reconstruction of those crossings remains uncertain at this resolution.

## Follow-up: selective outline mask

`selective_outline_repair.py` uses a 3-pixel band around the registered letter boundary and selects pixels with a strong local luminance extremum and a gradient along the boundary normal. A 7-pixel median gives the local reference. Three thresholds selected 4,332, 6,256, or 9,779 pixels; OpenCV Telea filled only those pixels. The broadest mask removes more of the thin contour but smears adjacent detail, especially around the lower head and teeth. All three results still visibly read `SAMPLE`, because the letter interiors remain a different tone and texture from adjacent art. Thus a precise outline-only mask is possible, but outline repair alone is insufficient on this card. More complex local inpainting is unlikely to fix the broad interior mismatch without a better watermark model or reference art.

## Follow-up: preserving artwork from one image

The user's quality criterion is to preserve the original anatomy, even if some watermark residue or softness remains. No alternate version of this artwork is used in the result below. I tested three more source-only approaches:

- The [SLBR visible-watermark model](https://github.com/bcmi/SLBR-Visible-Watermark-Removal), using its official pretrained weights at 256 and 512 pixels, left the word and introduced blotches.
- A low-strength diffusion inpainting pass conditioned on the de-whitened pixels preserved the face, but reinforced the residual `SAMPLE` pattern. This exposed a fidelity/removal tradeoff: when the model sees the residual letters, it tends to keep them; when they are entirely masked, it invents anatomy.
- A small alpha-estimation CNN trained only on clean regions of this card with simulated translucent text learned the inverse blend, but still left the word because its approximate mask and synthetic overlay did not exactly match the actual watermark.

The better source-only approach is `one_image_poisson.py`. It renders an approximate `SAMPLE` font mask using a manually estimated bounding box, aligns that mask to *gradients in the input image*, then solves a Poisson equation over the letter pixels. Inside each letter it preserves observed image gradients, scaled to compensate for a 0.60-opacity overlay. It discards gradients in a roughly five-pixel band around each letter edge, where the watermark dominates, and anchors the solution to untouched pixels outside the mask. The resulting [`one_image_result.png`](archive/root-images/one_image_result.png) no longer reads as `SAMPLE` at card size and keeps the original lower head, teeth, and fur layout. On this machine, alignment plus reconstruction took 3.52 seconds on CPU. Exactly 53,161 pixels fell inside the mask; a pixel comparison found **zero changes outside it**.

This is still not exact recovery: within the letters, contrast and WebP artifacts can be amplified, while the narrow edge band must be interpolated. The current result is softer in the marked area, especially on red fur and the lower head. The box and font were supplied for this sample, so automatic word detection and mask initialization remain application work. A narrower edge band retains slightly more detail but leaves visible letter contours. This source-only method is presently the best match for fidelity on this test image.

## Follow-up: missing lower-eye highlight

The visible yellow highlight at approximately (426, 371) lies directly on the left edge of a watermark letter. Its input pixel is RGB (255, 229, 185), but the first Poisson output was (113, 60, 82). The solver deliberately discarded gradients across a several-pixel letter border, so it erased this compact feature along with the watermark edge. This is the same mechanism that causes some softness in the former word area; the original watermark also attenuates fine detail and the lossy WebP has already compressed it.

The first update to `one_image_poisson.py` detected compact bright features with chroma distinct from their immediate surroundings. It used a local luminance peak and CIELAB chroma difference, with **no eye coordinates, object labels, or external artwork**. For detected pixels, it added a soft color constraint from the inverse-overlay estimate to the Poisson solve. This restored the prominent right-eye glow, but missed the smaller left-eye highlight: the watermark desaturates that highlight enough to fall below the first chroma threshold. The original image shows a small yellow spot where the top of the `P` crosses the left eye; the first revised output still lacked it.

The second update also requires negative image curvature in both directions, distinguishing compact bright blobs from long watermark strokes. That allows a lower chroma threshold without selecting most letter edges. It found 16 high-confidence pixels in two compact clusters corresponding to the two visible eye highlights. [`one_image_result_both_eyes.png`](archive/root-images/one_image_result_both_eyes.png) restores the left glow and retains the right. Differences from the previous output are localized mainly around the left eye; pixels outside the original 53,161-pixel repair mask remain byte-identical. This is automatic detection, but the exact color and brightness of partly obscured highlights remain estimates.

To check whether the rule only fits this card, `validate_feature_anchors.py` applied simulated translucent white `SAMPLE` marks to three unrelated images bundled with scikit-image (astronaut, cat, coffee), at three placements each. Against the known clean originals, the revised two-eye anchor rule lowered mean absolute error inside the repair region in seven cases and left two unchanged; none worsened. This is a limited synthetic check, not proof that real watermarks on arbitrary images will be cleanly removed. Achromatic highlights may not trigger the color-based safeguard, and the broader Poisson method still produces visible artifacts on some photographic scenes. The remaining softness motivated the texture recovery experiment below.

## Follow-up: recovering texture in the watermark area

The earlier solve assumed 0.60 white opacity. Comparing the marked pixels with the Poisson estimate gave a median per-channel opacity of about 0.69–0.71 in safe letter interiors. At 0.60, the solver under-amplified the surviving artwork gradients, making the repaired fur and teeth flatter. Raising the value to 0.70 restored more contrast. A direct inverse blend in each letter interior added further source texture, while the Poisson result remains at letter edges where slight mask misregistration would leave visible outlines. The blend is capped at 75% and fades in from two to five pixels inside each glyph. It is suppressed near the preserved eye highlights.

The new [`one_image_result_texture.png`](one_image_result_texture.png) retains both eye glows and has visibly sharper fur, teeth, and red body texture than [`one_image_result_both_eyes.png`](archive/root-images/one_image_result_both_eyes.png). The paired crop is [`texture_recovery_comparison.png`](archive/root-images/texture_recovery_comparison.png). Mean grayscale gradient magnitude in a safe inner region rose from 42.99 to 55.99. This number indicates restored contrast, but does not establish that every fine detail is correct. Exactly zero pixels outside the 53,161-pixel repair mask changed relative to the supplied original.

`validate_texture_recovery.py` tested the blend on three unrelated bundled images with synthetic 0.70-opacity text, WebP quality 80 compression, a 0.3-pixel mask shift, and assumed opacities of 0.68, 0.70, and 0.72. The blend lowered mean absolute error in all nine cases compared with the corresponding Poisson result (for example, astronaut at assumed 0.70: 16.39 to 11.04). This supports the conservative blend but does not guarantee the same result for other fonts, overlay colors, images, or larger registration errors. Some texture remains estimated or lost at letter edges.

## Two-pass opacity and matte refinement test

Tested that hypothesis on both real cards. A first pass used the established method and emitted its aligned matte. `matte_refinement_probe.py` then estimated white-overlay opacity from safe glyph interiors by comparing each supplied input with its first-pass reconstruction. The median estimates were **0.6990 for OP18-021** and **0.6999 for OP18-085**, so the existing 0.70 setting was already close for both images.

The probe also estimated a per-pixel edge matte from the same input and first-pass result, discounted pixels whose RGB channels disagreed with a white-overlay model, and blended that estimate with the rendered mask. A second Poisson pass with two blend strengths (0.5 and 1.0) produced `archive/root-images/OP18-021_refined_05.png`, `archive/root-images/OP18-021_refined_10.png`, and counterparts for OP18-085. See `archive/root-images/matte_refinement_comparison.png`. The masks and thousands of pixel values changed, but there was **no clear visual improvement** on either card: dark and soft patches remained on OP18-021. All outputs had zero changed pixels outside their respective refined repair masks. The original first-pass output was byte-identical to the established result on each card.

This test weakens the idea that a wrong *global* opacity is the main cause of the second card's artifacts. It does not rule out a better edge model: the refined matte depends on a first-pass reconstruction that already contains errors, so it can reproduce those errors. The probe is retained as an experiment; the baseline output remains preferred.

## Controlled error-source experiment

`diagnose_error_sources.py` constructs test canvases only from unmarked upper-art regions of the two supplied cards, applies a known 70%-opacity `SAMPLE` matte, optionally encodes to lossy WebP, then runs the same reconstruction. These stretched clean regions are diagnostic ground truth, not alternate artwork used to produce either real-card result. Mean absolute RGB error inside the true watermark area was:

| Clean region | No compression, exact matte | WebP quality 80, exact matte | WebP 80, matte shifted 1 px | WebP 80, matte shifted 2 px | WebP quality 60, exact matte |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| OP18-021 | 6.61 | 9.91 | 10.03 | 12.02 | 11.31 |
| OP18-085 | 5.06 | 7.58 | 7.59 | 10.54 | 8.50 |

At this synthetic watermark size, lossy compression caused a larger error increase than a 0.3- or 1-pixel mask shift; a 2-pixel shift was also costly. Even with exact matte and no compression, errors remained near glyph edges because the Poisson method discards uncertain edge gradients. This test does not reproduce all real watermark export details, so its ranking is evidence about the method, not a measured decomposition of the real card's artifacts.

I also tested non-local-means denoising of the observed image before reconstruction at strengths 2, 4, and 6. It increased error for both synthetic cards at every strength (OP18-021 WebP 80: 9.91 baseline versus 11.08, 13.07, and 14.85). Strength 2 visibly erased some detail on both real results; see `archive/root-images/denoise_comparison.png`. The optional denoising code was removed. A small bilateral filter applied to the reconstructed repair area lowered synthetic error only marginally (9.91 to 9.78 on OP18-021 and 7.58 to 7.37 on OP18-085) and made almost no visible improvement on the real cards; see `archive/root-images/bilateral_comparison.png`. Neither is a convincing fix.

## Region-aware cleanup probes

`flat_region_transfer_probe.py` tried to copy colors from nearby unmasked, locally flat pixels of the same supplied input. The transfer was gated by gradients and distance from visible edges. On the 2× OP13-012 result, three spatial weights changed many pixels but yielded little visible improvement and retained broad residual bands (`archive/root-images/flat_transfer_comparison.png`). ## Automatic rough-box detection

`detect_watermark_box.py` now searches the whole image for the oriented edge pattern of a rendered known word and font, across a range of sizes. It returns a rough box; the existing gradient alignment in `one_image_poisson.py` then refines that box. The PowerShell wrapper `Remove-Watermark.ps1` runs this detector by default, shows the box for review, and accepts an explicit `-Box` override. Direct Python use can pass `--auto-box`. The detector searches for a **known word**, not arbitrary unknown watermark content.

On four supplied marked images, the detector returned boxes close to the manually supplied ones: OP18-085 `(49,355,549,488)`, OP18-021 `(47,353,557,489)`, OP13-012 1× `(53,375,587,517)`, and OP13-012 2× `(107,751,1173,1035)`. Their match scores were 1.07, 0.794, 0.792, and 0.797. Two synthetic clean canvases made from unmarked card regions scored 0.266 and 0.154, so the wrapper treats scores below 0.5 as uncertain and requires a manual box. This is a limited check, not a calibrated confidence guarantee for arbitrary fonts, watermark colors, or image layouts. Reconstruction with detected boxes looked comparable to the corresponding manually boxed results (`archive/root-images/autobox_output_comparison.png` and `archive/root-images/OP18-085_autobox_comparison.png`).

## Processing order: remove first, then upscale

The local Upscayl standard-4x model is available as a Windows command-line tool. I compared two orders using the same supplied source images and model: (1) upscale the marked image to 2× and run `one_image_poisson.py`, and (2) run the source-only reconstruction at original size, then upscale the repaired result to 2×. On both OP18-085 and OP18-021, order (2) visibly reduced letter-shaped tone patterns and produced smoother, more coherent repaired texture (`archive/root-images/processing_order_comparison.png`). The improvement is strongest on OP18-085, where the residual `SAMPLE` is no longer readily readable in the 2× result. OP18-021 still has some localized artifacts. This is a visual comparison on two cards, not a general benchmark.

Upscayl changes pixels throughout its output. To preserve the user's 2× source outside the watermark repair area, `composite_upscaled_repair.py` copies the remove-then-upscale candidate only through the original repair mask scaled to 2× and expanded by 20 pixels, with a 16-pixel feather near its outer edge. The resulting `OP18-085_remove_then_upscale_composite.png` and `archive/root-images/OP18-021_remove_then_upscale_composite.png` changed **zero pixels outside their respective expanded masks** (306,295 and 310,210 pixels). The 2× OP18-085 composite is the best visual result on that card so far. `Remove-Then-Upscale.ps1` automates this workflow using the installed local Upscayl model, retaining intermediate files for inspection. ## Research note: residual SAMPLE outlines (13 Nov 2026)

The remaining marks should be treated as a **watermark formation-model error**, not only as a thin contour. The project already tested fixed-width edge interpolation, selective outline inpainting, global opacity sweeps, and a two-pass matte refinement; none removed the broad letter-shaped tone differences without damaging art. The best OP18-085 remove-then-upscale composite has only faint seams, whereas OP13-012 still has broad lost or shifted ornament. Those require different levels of reconstruction.

The highest-value next experiment is to calibrate the watermark's *actual per-pixel alpha matte and export process* before another reconstruction pass. The current program assumes an Impact glyph rendered by Pillow, one scalar opacity, and direct white compositing. Antialiasing, subpixel position, edge softness, color space, and lossy encoding can change the true observed letter profile. Fit a small parameterized forward model on synthetic watermarks applied to clean regions of the **same supplied image**, then test whether that fitted model predicts held-out marked edge profiles. For OP18-085/021, compare in linear RGB and sRGB, antialiasing kernels, subpixel shifts, and WebP encode/decode; keep the variant only if it lowers held-out synthetic error *and* the measured real-image watermark-correlated residual. The existing experiment found WebP compression more damaging than a small mask shift, so this check is particularly relevant.

If the same `SAMPLE` layer appears across several user-supplied cards, a second experiment can estimate a shared watermark matte from the aligned cards. This uses other cards only to estimate the shared watermark layer, never as replacement artwork for a target. Dekel et al.'s multi-image matting method supports this direction: https://watermark-cvpr17.github.io/ . With only two cards, confidence may be limited, so use it as a comparison to the single-image fit.

After improving the matte, run the existing Poisson reconstruction with a spatial confidence map: retain source-derived gradients in reliable stroke interiors, and repair only low-confidence edge and clipping pixels. A screened-Poisson or similar data term could constrain reconstructed colors where inverse compositing is reliable while retaining the boundary and artwork-gradient constraints. This is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated improvement; related gradient-domain formulation: https://www.ipol.im/pub/art/2014/84/ . A learned local residual stage is a later option only if the forward-model approach plateaus; recent watermark-removal research explicitly separates watermark suppression from background restoration and uses visible background content: https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.14383 and https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04687 . Our prior full-mask generative trials changed anatomy, so such a stage would need strict local compositing and a fidelity check.

Validation should use held-out synthetic marks on clean patches from multiple supplied images, including the original source format and lossy encoding, plus side-by-side inspection of the real Cerberus and Franky cards. Report repair-mask MAE/structure on synthetic truth, watermark-template correlation in real residuals, and changed pixels outside the repair mask. Preserve the current default unless a candidate clearly improves both synthetic and real results. Missing original detail under a strongly opaque letter cannot be recovered exactly from one composite.

### Vivi blurred-line diagnosis and narrower gradient band, 13 Nov 2026

The user pointed out that recovered lines still looked blurry. A likely algorithmic cause is the baseline Poisson solver's 7×7 erosion of the safe glyph core: it discards original source gradients within roughly three pixels of every letter boundary. This protects against watermark edges but also discards real gold ornament and dress-line gradients crossing those boundaries. The edge-recovery mode restored gradients fractionally but retained that wide exclusion band.

`edge_gradient_probe.py` compared 3×3, 5×5, 7×7, and 9×9 safe-core erosion on two independent synthetic indexed Vivi sections. ### One-command PowerShell runner, 13 Nov 2026

`Remove-With-Flux2.ps1` runs the source-only repair, automatic SAMPLE mask fitting, standard 2× Upscayl, local 4-bit Klein repaint, and strict final composite from one input path.
